    Bramble Brigade

    Five beds, one gate, and everything in the garden after your vegetables. Plant a brigade down the rows and hold the patch: Wellsprings make the water that everything else costs, Peppershots do the shooting, and a Bramble just refuses to move. The trick is that nothing stays the right answer for long — a snail's shell shrugs off ordinary shots, wasps fly clean over the whole ground line you spent two minutes building, moles tunnel under your front row and come up behind it, and an aphid clump bursts into two smaller ones, so killing it late is far worse than killing it early. Every row has a scarecrow that saves it once; after that, one pest through the gate and the week is over. Twenty-four levels across four gardens, and each garden changes a rule rather than just adding more: at dusk no water falls from the sky at all, the rockery puts stone exactly where you wanted to build, and on the compost heap any square that loses a plant turns sour and stays sour. Four bosses, a potting shed full of permanent upgrades, a button that calls the next wave in early for extra water if you fancy your chances, and a Long Summer that never stops coming.

    Aquamarine

    Two dice a turn, and you take one of them: draw a box that size onto the ocean, and whatever it encloses is yours. Taking the bigger die costs air, and so does going deep — one extra breath below the first line, two below the second. Three dives, three tanks, twenty-four turns, and a wheel that turns from day to night while you spend them, because the stingrays only count in daylight and the cuttlefish only after dark. The catch is the rule everybody forgets on paper: a new dive may not touch an earlier one, not even at a corner. Here the squares you may draw on light up before you drag, so it simply cannot happen. Five maps, each a different game underneath — caves you need a torch for, a trench you can only reach through a tunnel, ice that charges you for swimming beside it, and fossils you take by sighting a line straight through the rock. A digital version of the print-and-play by Postmark Games, designed by Matthew Dunstan and Rory Muldoon — made for us, not by them.

    Tiny Rex

    You hatch tiny, and there is only one question in the whole game: is that smaller than me? Eat it if it is. Keep away if it is not. Anything exactly your own size is completely safe — you bump noses and carry on — because nobody should lose a heart for failing to tell two similar shapes apart. Fill your belly and you grow a size, and every creature you spent the last minute running away from turns into dinner. Twenty hunts across four valleys: Fern Hollow, where nothing hunts you and you just learn the rule; Bone Gulch, where things start coming after you; Spike Ridge, where the armoured ones can never be eaten however small they look; and Thunder Basin, where the biggest animals alive live. Each valley ends on a named beast that hunts you until you out-grow it — and then it runs, sprinting faster than you can and stopping for breath, and that is when you get it. Stars come from your hearts and never from the clock, so you can take as long as you like. Berries and ferns are safe to eat at every size, so there is always something. Then there is the Endless Feast, where there is no target and no clock but your belly empties on its own, so you can never stop hunting — and a Dino Book that fills in with a card and a fact for every creature you meet.

    Curio Cabinet

    Turn over two drawers. If they match they stay open, and if they do not they go back down — but you got to see them, and that is the whole game. Twenty-five halls across five wings, and each wing starts on a smaller board than the last one finished, because the difficulty comes from the cards rather than the size: friendly animals in the Front Hall, then shells and pressed flowers, then a Star Room where nearly everything is small and round and glowing, then a workshop of identical brass, and finally a Reading Room of numbers and capital letters with no pictures to lean on at all. Every board flashes face-up for a moment before the clock starts, and the look gets shorter the deeper you go. Stars come from misses, never from the clock: finishing is one star, and the other two are for how few times you turned over two that did not go together. Time Attack starts on thirty seconds — every pair buys a little back, clearing the board buys more and deals a bigger one, and it ends when the boards outgrow the clock. Or hand the phone back and forth in a two-player game, where finding a pair means you go again.

    Updraft

    You cannot steer. A hot-air balloon has a burner and a vent, so up and down is the whole of your control — everything sideways is decided by which of six winds you happen to be sitting in, and they all blow different ways at different speeds. The entire stack is on screen at once, because picking a layer is the game. The top wind is the fastest and the air up there is so thin the burner drinks gas at double the rate, so speed costs fuel. Golden columns of rising air lift you for nothing, if you know where they are. Drop a sandbag and you leap upward — and you will never dive as fast again, so spend them carefully. Birds tear the envelope and every tear makes it cool quicker for the rest of the flight, so a bad patch gets worse. And arriving is not landing: sail over the meadow in a fast wind and you have to find something westerly and come all the way back. Twenty flights over meadows, chalk downs, a storm coast and a mountain pass, a loft full of fittings bought with ribbons, and a Long Drift that generates one endless sky a day — the same sky for everyone in the house.

    Cookie Crumble

    Run a village tearoom, on your own, with an owl for a waitress. Show people to a table, take the order, put the urn and the oven on, load your tray at the pass and get it out before anybody gives up waiting. Every job is one tap and none of them is hard — what costs you is that you have to walk there, and you can only be in one place. Six tables end up live at once in five different states and there is always something going cold. Eleven regulars each break your routine in exactly one way: the fox who tips double and will not wait, the squirrels who leave crumbs everywhere, the badgers who will sit all afternoon, the hedgehog who wants nobody near him, the deer who cannot bear to sit alone, the cat who wanted the other tablecloth, the frog who orders again once he has finished, the birthday bear who wants it all in one trip — and an inspector who will shut you down if she leaves unhappy. Seat somebody on their own colour of tablecloth for a bonus, do the same job twice running for a multiplier, and spend the takings on quicker shoes, a bigger tray and eighteen trims that go up in the room. Forty shifts, ten Big Days, and an endless Saturday Rush for the leaderboard.

    Bubble & Brew

    Reach into the bag and pull out an ingredient. Every chip stirs your potion further up the cauldron, and the points climb much faster near the top — but fizzroot fills the gauge, and one chip too many takes the lid off and the whole round with it. Stop whenever you like. Twenty recipes across five chapters, each with its own bag and its own apothecary: calmwort settles the fizz, thornvine catches a fizzroot outright, echobell doubles whatever comes next including its fizz, and quicksilver draws again whether you wanted it to or not. The coins survive a blow-up and the points don't, so exploding in round one is a shopping trip and exploding in round five is a disaster. There's a Bottomless Cauldron with one life for the leaderboard, and a Daily everyone plays with the identical bag.

    Number Ninja

    Numbers fly up from the dojo floor — slice the right ones and let the rest fall. Twelve belts take the 2 to 12 times tables one at a time and then all at once, asking them three ways: what's 7 × 8, what times 8 makes 56, and which of these are in the 7s. The wrong answers are the ones people actually give, so nothing can be guessed by eye. Get one wrong and you get a free think. Eleven dojo spirits grow as you master their tables — nothing to buy, only maths — and Sensei's Test puts the whole family on one leaderboard.

    Canary Deep

    Minesweeper, a thousand feet down a coal mine — and no board here ever needs a guess. Before you are dealt one, a solver plays it right through using logic alone, and if it would ever come down to a coin toss that board is thrown away and another is cut. Whatever square you are stuck on can be worked out, which is what lets the deep shifts run at gas densities well past classic Expert without ever being unfair. Eighteen shifts down three seams, from an easy day hole to a last one that is more than a quarter gas. You carry a canary, and it does one of two jobs: send it ahead and it lands on a square you could already have proved safe, or leave it in the cage and it takes the hit the first time you tap gas. Insurance or information, and you only get one. Plus a Daily Seam the whole family plays on the same board.

    Scribble Speedway

    Draw a racetrack with your finger and the game turns your scribble into a real circuit — smoothing the wobbles, rounding off the corners, nudging the tarmac clear of the trees — and then you race it. Eighteen challenges across four chapters, each one a field of ponds, boulders and golden rings to design a circuit through, and then a full grid of seven rivals waiting on it. The corners are the whole game: the outside of a bend can be taken faster and the inside covers less ground, so there's a racing line to find on a track nobody has ever driven. Auto-brake for smaller drivers, coins for the garage, and a Daily Circuit where the whole family gets the same field and each draws their own way round it. Save your favourites to the Track Book and your best lap becomes a ghost car for everyone else to chase.
